How it works when you're mid-job

The mechanic is simple:

  1. A call rings out. You're on the wand halfway down a driveway, up a roof, or rinsing a house wash. The phone sits in the ute, ringing.
  2. The workflow fires. The platform sees the call end unanswered the moment it happens.
  3. An SMS lands in about a minute. It comes from your business number and reads like you: "Sorry I missed your call, still after a price on the driveway? Reply here and I'll get back to you shortly."
  4. The caller is saved as a contact. Their number lands in one inbox, tagged as a missed call, not vanished off your recent-calls list.
  5. Follow-up runs itself. If they reply, you pick it up when you're off the roof. If they go quiet, a polite sequence chases the job for you.

The Spam Act 2003 part, in plain English

Australia's Spam Act 2003 covers commercial SMS, so give this two minutes. In the normal setup, the caller dialled your number first. A single reply text about their enquiry sits under what the Act calls inferred consent: they initiated contact with you, and your reply relates to exactly that. You're on solid ground. Three habits keep you there:

  • Identify your business. The text should make clear who it's from, not read like a mystery number.
  • Stay on the enquiry. Reply about their job. Don't pivot the same thread into a promo blast.
  • Honour the stop. If they reply "no" or "stop", the sequence ends immediately, no exceptions.

What you can't do is harvest every missed caller into a marketing list and text them months later; that needs its own opt-in. General information, not legal advice: ACMA publishes the plain-English rules.
